Why This Lab Feels Hard

This lab looks simple at first. It is only asking whether someone can commute. But the task quickly becomes difficult because it does not ask one question. It asks several questions at once, and each question depends on the answer to an earlier one. That is why this tutorial exists. We are not going to start by staring at code. We are going to start with a story. The story will help us see the shape of the problem before we translate that shape into Python.

Meet Big Bob

Big Bob is nearing retirement. He lives with his wife Claire on a small farm they bought together when they were 21. They love it there. It's known locally as Tan House, because the building is old and has exposed timber frames, with wattle and daub walls between them. It is a little paradise. Bob has three regular journeys. Before we get distracted by how he makes them, let's just look at where they go: His favourite Auntie's house — less than a mile away. The local store and gas station — about 4 miles away. Town, where Bob works at the lumber yard — about 8 miles away. Three destinations. Three distances. That's it for now.

It's tempting to immediately ask "how does Bob get to each one?" — but hold that thought. Before we worry about bikes, cars, or neighbours, the distance alone already tells us something: a trip to Auntie's is not the same kind of problem as a trip to town. They don't just differ in number of miles. They differ in what's even possible. Walking eight miles to the lumber yard isn't a real option. Driving the quarter-mile to Auntie's would be a bit daft. The distance itself sorts the three journeys into three different kinds of decision before a single mode of transport gets mentioned. So: hold the three distances in your head as their own thing for a moment.

Now, the transport. Bob doesn't have unlimited choices for each trip — what's available depends on which journey he's making. Auntie's house is close enough to walk, and Bob likes the walk. She's locally famous for her pork pies, beer, and wine, so the trip is its own reward — assuming he can still walk in a straight line on the way back. The store is a different matter — too far to bother walking, but well within range of Bob's old bike, which is fitted with a big basket on the front and pannier bags on the back. It keeps him fit, which suits Claire fine, since she wants him healthy and happy, in that order if it comes to it. Town is a different matter again. Eight miles is too far for both the old bike and the old Bob. For that, Bob has a car, and he also has a standing arrangement with his neighbour Albert: they take turns driving each other to work, and between them they even pick up a small yearly tax credit for sharing the commute.

So far, so sensible. Except the region is hilly, and prone to sudden, heavy rain — the kind that arrives without much warning and leaves just as fast, but not before doing some damage. Once, Bob was caught out on the bike, blown off course in a sudden downpour, and ended up in a ditch that had, with impressive timing, turned itself into a small lake. He dried out eventually. His knee did not, fully. Claire was not impressed, and has not stopped mentioning it. That's the whole problem in miniature: distance decides what's even on the table, and then the weather decides whether the things on the table are a good idea.

We like Bob. He's always interested in what we're learning, so we've decided to help him out — and ourselves — by building him a Travel and Weather app, so he can ask it a question before he ends up in a ditch again. Not a grand, futuristic app. We're students. Just something that can answer one question:

Can Bob commute today?

The Story Questions

From the story, we can ask: Where is Bob going? Is it raining? Does Bob have a bike? Does Bob have a car? Does Bob have access to ride-share? Those questions come from Bob's life. Now we connect them to the lab's questions.

Distance Chooses the Chapter

Bob's journeys create distance categories: Auntie's house: 1 mile or less. The store: greater than 1 mile and up to 6 miles. Town: greater than 6 miles. This is the first big idea of the lab. These categories aren't just background facts about Bob's life — they're the chapters of the app's rulebook. Each chapter decides which smaller question gets asked next.

If Bob is going to Auntie's, the app opens the short-trip chapter and asks about rain. If Bob is going to the store, it opens the medium-trip chapter and asks about bike plus rain. If Bob is going to town, it opens the long-trip chapter and asks about car or ride-share.

Short trip: Bob can go only if it is not raining. Medium trip: Bob can go only if he has a bike and it is not raining. Long trip: Bob can go only if he has a car or ride-share access.

So distance isn't just another variable sitting in the list. Distance is the organiser of the rulebook. Distance chooses the chapter — and the other variables only matter once that chapter has been chosen. If Bob is only going to Auntie's, we only need to ask about rain. If he's going to the store, we need bike and rain. If he's going to town, we need car or ride-share.

Distance decides which rulebook opens. The rulebook decides which question gets asked next. That's why we need to understand distance before we go anywhere near the code.

The Missing Question

At this point, it feels like we have all the questions. We don't. Have a look back and think about what key question is missing? Go back to the porch.

What if Bob isn't going anywhere today?

If Bob has no journey, the rest of the app has nothing to decide. Worse, it has nothing to fall back on — no exit, no default, nothing to do but break. That's a coder's nightmare: the case nobody planned for. It doesn't matter whether Bob has a bike. It doesn't matter whether the store is four miles away. It doesn't matter whether Albert's free for a lift. If there's no usable distance, there's no commute — and no commute means none of the rulebook chapters even get opened.

That gives us the master question, the one sitting quietly underneath all the others:

Does Bob have a usable distance?

Here's the uncomfortable bit. This question should have come first. Before short, medium, or long trip. Before rain, bike, car, or ride-share. But it's the one nobody asks first. In real life, you don't open a conversation about a trip by checking whether the trip exists. You just say where you're going. The going is assumed. Nobody starts a sentence with "assuming I am in fact leaving the house" — that part is silent, baked into the fact that you're talking about a journey at all. Python doesn't let you get away with that.

if distance_mi already means "assume there's something here — now check." The checking is silent, same as in speech. But the moment you need the opposite case — the no-journey case, the cider-on-the-porch case — you can't just not say it. You have to say it backwards: if not distance_mi. Check whether the thing you'd normally assume is missing.

And that's exactly the sentence humans never build, because we don't ask questions about things we already know aren't happening. You don't stand on the porch and ask "am I, in fact, not going anywhere?" You just stay on the porch. The not-going is the default, so it never becomes a question in your head. Python has no defaults like that. It only has two boxes: true or false, asked or asked-backwards. So the case that feels too obvious to mention in English — Bob's simply not going anywhere — is the exact case that needs its own deliberate, backwards-sounding line of code: if not distance_mi. Not because the idea is complicated. Because English never had to say it out loud, and Python always does.

That's the thing about coding. The question that has to go first in the code is usually the last one you think to ask in real life.

So before any rulebook chapter, before any distance category, the app needs to check the porch first. That gives us the master question — the first thing we must ask:

Does Bob have a usable distance?

Notice we are still not writing code yet.

First, we need to understand what this question is really asking. In the story, Bob either has somewhere to go or he does not. If he is going to Auntie's, the store, or town, there is a distance for the app to work with. If he is staying home with Claire, there is no journey for the app to solve. That means the app needs to ask: Does the distance box have something useful inside it? This is where Python's if and if not become useful.

The 'if' and 'if not' Paradigm

Before we look at a single line of code, there is a shift of mind that has to happen, and it is worth doing slowly, because almost everyone trips on it the first time and nobody warns them in advance. Every human language we grow up speaking gives us two separate tools for answering a question: yes, and no. They are not the same tool wearing different clothes — they are genuinely two different words, two different concepts, sitting side by side in the language from the start. "Is it raining?" "No." "Do you have a bike?" "No." We never had to build the no-answer out of the yes-answer. It simply existed, already its own thing, ready whenever we needed it.

That works because humans think. When you ask someone a question, they consider it, weigh it against what they know, and choose a response — yes, no, or something in between. "No" is a genuine act of cognition. The person understood the question, understood the situation, decided the answer was negative, and said so.

Python does not weigh the situation as a person would. It evaluates the condition as truthy or falsy, then follows the instruction you wrote. A plain if has one visible action: when the condition is truthy, run the indented block. When the condition is falsy, skip that block and continue.

The green-light model: picture the block beneath if as having one green go-light. A truthy condition turns it on and the block runs. A falsy condition leaves it dark and that block is skipped. Python can still represent False, and an else can handle the skipped route; the light is only a picture of what happens to this one block.

Now watch what happens when you need to check for absence — when the question you actually want to ask is "does Bob not have a bike?" The only signal Python has is a green go condition. Either something is there that matches the question, or something is not. So the question you write has to be framed so that the green go condition fires whichever aspect of the variable it is you care about. If you care about presence — the bike exists — you frame it this way:

if has_bike:

Green for go fires when the bike is there. But if you care about absence — the bike is missing — you need green to fire for the missing case instead. That means framing a different question:

if not has_bike:

This is not a trick. It is a differently framed question—one where the block runs for absence rather than presence. The mechanism is identical; what changed is the condition you asked Python to evaluate.

The green light is a learning picture, not Python terminology. The precise version is: Python evaluates a condition, runs the block when it is truthy, and skips it when it is falsy.

The same choice is available every time you write an if line: frame the question so green fires for the case you need to act on. if frames it for presence. if not frames it for absence. One tool, two framings.

Take a kettle:

kettle_full = True

if kettle_full — the question is framed around the kettle having water. Green for go fires when it does. Now suppose the kettle is empty and that is the case you need to catch. Frame the question the other way:

if not kettle_full

The question is now framed around the kettle not having water. Green fires when it doesn't. Notice what Python did not produce when kettle_full is True and this question ran: nothing. Not a red signal. Silence. The question was framed for the empty case; the kettle was full; green did not fire. That is the whole of it — and it is always the framing of the question, nothing else, that determines when green fires.

Try a switch:

light_on = True

if light_on — framed for the switch being on. if not light_on — framed for the switch being off. The word "off" does not exist in Python as a concept of its own. There is only light_on, and if or if not are the only choices available for asking about either state. English gave you the word "off" for free. Python never got that word. It only got green — and green is for go.

Now a third example: a door with a lock.

door_locked = True

In English, you have two natural questions about a locked door: "is it locked?" and "is it unlocked?" Two words, two concepts, two answers sitting ready. In Python, there is only one concept — door_locked — and only one signal — green. So: if door_locked — green fires if the door is locked. if not door_locked — green fires if the door is unlocked. The word "unlocked" does not exist in Python. There is no door_unlocked variable waiting to be checked. There is only door_locked, and if or if not are the only choices available for asking about either state.

This is what makes if not feel strange the first time: English always gave you a separate word for the opposite, so you never had to frame the question differently — the different word did it for you. Python never gave you that word. Every check for absence, emptiness, failure, the off state, the unlocked state, the missing bike — all of it has to be expressed by framing the question so that green fires for the absent case.

Once that is clear, if not stops being a grammatical oddity. It is just the absence-framing of the only question Python knows how to ask.

Note: For completeness — it is worth knowing that the green go condition is not limited just to True and False. Any value can fire a green go condition — a number, a piece of text, a list with something in it. Zero, an empty string, an empty list, and None all stay dark unless we use our if not operator on them — then they too will fire a green go condition. Booleans are simply the clearest case. The full picture is wider, and you will see it in action when numbers and comparisons arrive later in the tutorial.

The Importance of Being Backward

Here's a useful trick when you can't tell which question to ask first: don't look for it directly. Look at everything else you're about to ask, and find what they all secretly depend on. Take the full list of questions sitting in front of us: Is Bob going to Auntie's? Is Bob going to the store? Is Bob going to town? Is it raining? Does Bob have a bike? Does Bob have a car? Does Bob have ride-share access?

Every single one of those questions assumes something that isn't written down anywhere on the list: that Bob is going somewhere at all. Take that assumption away, and every question on the list becomes meaningless. There's no "is it raining" worth asking if Bob isn't leaving the porch. That's the tell. The question that everything else quietly leans on is the one that has to be asked first — even though it's the one nobody bothered to write down, because nobody thought to.

So the first question isn't found by staring harder at the variables. It's found by asking what job all the other variables are doing, and noticing that none of them can do their job until one earlier question has already been answered.

The Variable Conundrum

Before we write any code, there's one more thing worth understanding about variables. A variable has two sides split by the = sign in the middle:

score = 10

The right side is allowed to change. On different days it might hold any of these:

# it could be score = 10 # or later it could be score = 0 # tomorrow it could be score = 47

The value can change by the millisecond. What never changes is the name of the box — score — because the box itself is what the code is built around, not whatever happens to be sitting inside it at any given moment. This named box is the variable — not the number attached to it by the = sign. This matters because code doesn't run once. It runs for whatever values show up on the day. Change the number, and the code doesn't need to change at all. That's the whole point of giving something a name instead of just writing the number directly into your logic.

Five boxes. The numbers and the True/False values on the right can change from day to day — rain today, dry tomorrow — but the boxes themselves stay the same, because they're not really about today's weather or today's bike. They're named containers standing in for the story. The app never touches Bob's actual life. It only ever touches these five boxes. The app does not work with the story directly. The app works with logical containers as named variables that represent the story.

The Coding Principle

Two things are now nailed down. Questions create the structure — the if and if not shape of a check. Variables provide the state — the named boxes holding today's facts. There's a third piece still missing: something to join smaller questions into bigger ones. That's coming next. For now, hold the shape of the whole idea:

A Quick Note About Python Tools

You may already have noticed that certain words in this tutorial appear in different colours. That is not decoration. Each colour has one job and one job only: keywords like if, not, and, or appear in one colour; variable names appear in another; Python builtins like print() appear in a third. The colours are there to make the structure of the code visible at a glance, so you can see what kind of word you are looking at before you work out what it means.

The code words we use in this lab do not all do the same kind of job.

Some words build the decision path: if starts a decision. It asks the first question. elif continues the same decision. It means: if the earlier question did not pass, try this next question. else closes the decision. It means: if none of the earlier questions passed, use this final fallback answer. Together, if, elif, and else form one decision block. Python reads that block from top to bottom. The first question that passes wins, and Python skips the rest of that same block.

Some words change the shape of a question: not asks the has-not version of the question. Some values answer a question: True means the check passes. False means the check does not pass. Some functions show an answer: print() displays the result. Some operators connect smaller questions: and means all connected checks must pass. or means at least one connected check must pass.

So when we read code, we should not treat every Python word as the same kind of thing. First ask: what job is this word doing in the decision?

A Practical View Of True And False

Before going further, it is worth being precise about what True and False actually are — because we have been using True and False to practice thinking, yet the same two words do three completely different jobs at three different points in the process, and that is where the confusion usually starts.

The first job: state. When you write has_bike = True, True is not an answer to a question. It is a description of the current condition of the world — the switch position. Think of it as "present" rather than "true." has_bike = True means the bike exists right now. is_raining = False means rain is not currently happening. Nothing is being decided. Nothing is firing. These values just sit in their boxes describing the state of things.

The second job: input to the green go condition. When Python hits if has_bike, it looks inside the box and finds True. That is the input that makes the green light fire. If it finds False, the green light stays dark. Python is not producing True or False here — it is reading the state you put in the box and deciding whether to light green. True and False are what go in. Green firing or not firing is what comes out. They are at different points in the process. At this stage True and False are not words printed on screen — they are internal states that only Python can see and act on. Nothing appears on screen at all. The green light fires or stays dark, silently, and Python moves on.

The third job: display. When the lab asks you to write print(False), False appears to be an output. And it is — but it is the output of print(), not the output of if. The if already did its job and fired green. Then, because the green light fired, the next instruction ran — which happened to be print(False). The print instruction is simply displaying the word False on screen so the user knows the result. You could equally write print("No journey today") and it would work the same way. False inside print() is a display choice, not a signal from Python.

Same two words. Three completely different jobs. Keeping them separate in your head — state, input, display — is what stops this section from becoming a blur.

For this lab, distance_mi = 8 means Bob has a distance, so the distance question has something to work with. But if Bob is not going anywhere, the distance might be empty or zero. Then the app needs to catch that first. We can think of such key turning points as doorways to the next step, or as gated turnstiles that allow us to pass through to the next step. Understanding how these gates work, and how to structure them, will make the coding much easier.

Doorways — The Gate To Successful Code

A gate is a doorway in the logic of a program. Think of it as a turnstile, that either allows you to pass through it, or stops you from passing through it. It is the place where the program asks one simple question, then decides what should happen next. A gate does not do everything. It only decides whether the program is allowed to move through to the next part of the rulebook.

That sounds like a small thing to say about a single line of code, but it is the whole reason gates exist. If one line tried to ask every question at once — is there a journey, and is it short, and is it raining, and is there a bike — you could not read it, and neither could the person debugging it six months from now. A gate's job is deliberately narrow: ask one thing, then get out of the way. That narrowness is what keeps the code from becoming one tangled thought.

Each gate hands off to the next only if the gate before it did not already stop the program. You are never holding more than one question in your head at a time — you are holding a sequence of doorways, each one simple on its own, that together make something that would be unreadable if written as a single block.

Picture each gate as a turnstile with one light on it — green. The turnstile asks its one question. If the condition is met, the green light comes on and the turnstile unlocks — you walk through to the next one. If the condition is not met, the light stays dark and execution stops there. The turnstile does not turn and you cannot pass. There is no red light. Dark is not a signal. Dark is silence — the same silence Python produces whenever a condition fails to fire. The Gates, In Order — Most Disqualifying First

Gates are a way of getting our questions in the right order logically — we can write them out in our normal language. We don't need code to understand what must come first, and what must be asked after that. However, the order in which we ask questions matters as much as the questions themselves. If we ask the wrong question first, we waste time checking things that cannot possibly matter yet. The rule is simple: the question that, if it fails, makes every other question pointless — that one goes first.

Gate 1: Does Priya even want to borrow a book? If not: nothing to decide here — stop and say so. This is the gate that makes every other question worth asking at all.

Gate 2: Does Priya have a library card? If not: stop — she cannot borrow without one, regardless of what's on the shelf.

Gate 3: Is the book actually on the shelf? If not: stop — even with a card and a request, there is nothing to hand over.

Final: all three gates cleared. Priya can borrow the book.

Notice where Gate 1 sits. It is not "does she have a card" — that is Gate 2. It is not "is the book available" — that is Gate 3. Gate 1 is the question that, if the answer is no, makes Gates 2 and 3 completely pointless to even ask. There is no reason to check whether Priya has a card if she has not actually asked to borrow anything. There is no reason to check the shelf if there is no transaction to complete. Gate 1 is the question that earns the right to ask all the others.

That is the principle: order the gates so the most disqualifying check goes first. The most disqualifying check is always the one that, if it fails, makes every other question in the list pointless.

One Go Signal, Two Questions — The Importance of Framing Questions

This is where if and if not connect directly to the turnstile idea. Picture each turnstile with a single green go-light on it — the same green we introduced earlier. That light has one job: fire when the condition is met, stay dark when it is not. What determines which outcome happens is the question you frame for it. if has_coin frames the question around the coin being present — the green go-light fires when the coin is there. if not has_coin frames the question around the coin being absent — the green go-light fires when the coin is missing. The turnstile has not changed. The coin has not changed. What changed is which condition you asked the green light to confirm.

That is all not does. It does not flip the gate or reverse the logic. It reframes the question so the green go-signal fires for the opposite case. Once you see it that way, if not stops looking like a double negative and starts looking like exactly what it is: the same turnstile, asked a differently-shaped question.

Picture a vending machine. Before it dispenses anything, it has to pass through its own doorways, in order: Gate 1: has any coin been inserted at all? Gate 2: is the coin the right value? Gate 3: is the selected item actually in stock? Gate 4: has the dispense mechanism actually moved — did the chocolate bar physically drop? Four turnstiles, four lights. Every single one has to show green before the machine does the one thing it exists to do. Miss any of them — including the last one, the one nobody thinks to check — and there is no chocolate bar.

Notice the order is not arbitrary. There is no point checking the coin's value if no coin has been inserted — that question would not even make sense yet. And there is no point checking stock if the coin was wrong, because the machine has already decided the transaction is over. Each gate only gets asked because every gate before it passed: turnstile 1's green light is what earns the right to ask turnstile 2's question at all. If any turnstile stays dark, the rest never get asked — there is nothing left to check.

Here is the part most people writing their first vending machine program forget: Gate 4 exists because Gates 1 through 3 can all turn green and the chocolate bar still does not arrive. The coin was real, the value was right, the bar was in stock — and the mechanism jammed anyway. If your code only checks coin, value, and stock, and then just assumes the dispense happened because nothing told it otherwise, you have written a program that lies. It will cheerfully report success on a machine that just ate someone's dollar.

This is the gate everyone who has ever lost a dollar to a vending machine has felt with their whole body and never once thought to code for. You put the coin in. The light even said the right price. You picked the chocolate bar. Nothing came out. No bar, no refund, no apology — just a machine that took your money and went quiet. That feeling, the dull, specific unfairness of it, is what happens when a program checks three gates, gets three green lights, and skips the fourth. The code did not crash. It did not error. The 'if' And 'if not' Questions

By now you have seen if and if not in several examples, and you have felt the oddness of it — English never made you ask a question backwards to get a negative answer, but Python does. There is a name for the habit this forces on you, and it is worth naming properly before you write your first gate for Bob.

We call it positive interrogation. The idea is simple: Python only has one shape of question. It can only ever fire green for the case you ask about. So instead of hunting for a Python word that means "no" — there isn't one — you frame the question so that the go signal fires for whichever case you actually need to act on. If you need to act on presence, you write if. If you need to act on absence, you write if not. Same turnstile. Same go signal. Different question.

That means in practice we always ask clear, direct questions:

if distance_mi

Does distance_mi have a value?

if not distance_mi

Does distance_mi not have a value? The plain if question works when a variable has a useful value. The if not question is the check for the no-value case — empty, zero, missing, not useful for this decision.

Indentation: What Belongs To The Question

In Python, a line ending with : opens a block. Everything indented underneath that line belongs to that block — it only runs if the question above it fired. This is not a style choice. It is the mechanism Python uses to know what belongs to what. Without indentation, Python cannot tell which instructions are inside the gate and which come after it.

if not distance_mi: print(False)

Read it aloud: If distance_mi does not have a usable value — print False. The if not line is the question. The colon opens the block. The indented print line is the answer — and it only runs because the question above it fired green. The computer has already made the decision by the time it reaches print. The print instruction does not decide anything. It simply shows the result on screen.

That distinction matters. The gate fires or it doesn't. The print just reports what happened.

Now, print(False) and print("False") look identical in the output — both display the word False on screen. But Python understands them differently:

if not distance_mi: print(False) # Boolean value — Python knows this as True or False print("False") # text string — Python treats this as characters to display

False without quote marks is a Boolean — a value Python can reason about. print("False") with quote marks is text — characters to be displayed, nothing more. Both look the same to a human reading the output. Python sees them as completely different things before it gets that far. In this lab, the tests expect the Boolean — so print(False), not print("False").

Indentation is not decoration. It is how Python knows that print belongs to the gate above it. Use four spaces for each level, and keep every line in the same block aligned. The practical rule is: the indented line belongs inside the decision above it.

Use 'and' And 'or' Before The Gates Get Harder

Before the next set of gates asks you to check two things at once, let's get comfortable with and and or properly. Every gate so far has asked one question with one answer. The gates ahead need to ask two questions at once and combine the answers into a single go signal. That combining is the whole job of and and or, so it is worth slowing right down here, away from Bob's commute, with examples small enough to hold in your head without effort.

Think about a car. Before it will start, two things have to be true at once: the key has to be in the ignition, and the driver's seatbelt has to be fastened. Neither one on its own is enough. In Python, that looks like this:

key_in_ignition = True seatbelt_on = True if key_in_ignition and seatbelt_on: print("Car can start")

Read the if line as a single question with two parts: is the key in the ignition, AND is the seatbelt on? Both parts have to fire green, or the whole gate stays dark. Try it with the seatbelt undone:

key_in_ignition = True seatbelt_on = False if key_in_ignition and seatbelt_on: print("Car can start")

The gate stays dark. The key being in is irrelevant on its own — and does not care how close you got, only whether every part passed. One missing part and the whole gate stays dark, the same way one missing leg collapses a table that needs all of them.

Now think about a smoke alarm. It only needs one of two things to go off: smoke in the room, or the test button being pressed.

smoke_detected = False test_button_pressed = True if smoke_detected or test_button_pressed: print("Alarm sounds")

Read that as: is there smoke, OR was the test button pressed? The gate fires, because at least one part came back yes. or does not ask for agreement from both sides — it only asks whether at least one thing showed up. Even with smoke_detected staying False, the alarm still sounds because test_button_pressed alone was enough to carry the gate.

Put the two side by side and the difference becomes a feeling, not just a rule:

if key_in_ignition and seatbelt_on: # a checklist — every box must be ticked if smoke_detected or test_button_pressed: # a tripwire — one trigger is enough

and is what you reach for when missing even one thing makes the whole situation unworkable — like the car. or is what you reach for when any single thing happening is already enough to act on — like the alarm. Neither is "stricter" or "looser" in the abstract; each is built for a different shape of real-world requirement. Your job is to recognise which shape you are looking at before you write the line.

That recognition is the actual skill here, more than the keywords themselves. Whenever you are about to write and or or, stop and ask the question in plain English first: does this situation need every part to be true, or does it only need one? The car needed every part. The alarm needed only one. Once you can answer that about the real-world situation, choosing between and and or stops being a guess.

The Conditional Sandwich

Claire is a great sandwich maker. She builds Bob's favourite the same way every time: bottom slice first, then as many fillings as the sandwich needs, then the top slice to close it. You cannot start with the top slice. You cannot skip the bottom. And the fillings only make sense because they are held in place by the bread on both sides.

A conditional block in Python works exactly the same way. if is the bottom slice — it opens the block and asks the first question. else is the top slice — it closes the block and catches whatever is left. In between, you can stack as many elif fillings as the situation needs. Each one is another question, asked only if every question above it has already failed to fire. The first filling that fires wins, and Python skips the rest.

elif is short for "else if" — or if you prefer, "if else." Think of it as: if the previous question did not match, else try this one instead. You can have one elif, or ten, or none at all — the sandwich works with any number of fillings, as long as it always starts with if and closes with else.

For Bob's travel app, the sandwich looks like this — written in plain English first, before we worry about Python syntax:

if Bob has no usable distance: no commute elif Bob is going 1 mile or less: open the short-trip rulebook elif Bob is going up to 6 miles: open the bike-distance rulebook else: open the long-distance rulebook

One ordered block. Python reads it from top to bottom. The first branch that matches is the one that runs — and once it runs, the rest of the sandwich is skipped. That is how the program chooses which chapter of the rulebook to open. Shortly, we will translate each of these lines into real Python. But first — one more thing the sandwich needs to be able to do.

elif and the Comparison Operator

You have already met elif and know that it only fires if everything above it has already failed. There is one more thing it can do that you need for the exercises ahead: ask whether a number falls inside a range, using a comparison operator.

The one we need is <= — less than or equal to. Read it as: is this value smaller than, or exactly equal to, the number on the right? Here is a fresh example — a vending machine that sells different things depending on how much money is inserted:

vending_coins = 30 if vending_coins <= 10: print("Gum") elif vending_coins <= 50: print("Chocolate bar") else: print("Crisps")

Read it line by line. Is thirty less than or equal to ten? No — move on. Is thirty less than or equal to fifty? Yes — print "Chocolate bar" and stop. The else never gets asked, because the elif already fired.

Notice the second branch does not need to ask greater than ten as well. It doesn't, because the if branch already caught everything up to and including ten. By the time Python reaches the elif, it is already in a world where coins are more than ten — that lower boundary was established the moment the first branch failed. So you only ever ask the upper boundary. The sandwich handles the rest.

Why This Decision Needs No Final else

It can feel as though every conditional sandwich must end with else. This example shows why that is not a rule. No combination of True and False for cider_on_table and wine_on_table can pass all three gates without being handled.

The reason is straightforward. Both variables are boolean — they can only be True or False. Work through every possible combination:

# cider False, wine False → Gate 1 catches it (no drinks ready) # cider True, wine True → Gate 2 catches it (both drinks ready) # cider True, wine False → Gate 3 catches it (at least one drink ready) # cider False, wine True → Gate 3 catches it (at least one drink ready)

That is every possible combination exhausted. By the time Python reaches the else, all four cases have already been handled. The else has nothing left to catch.

So should we write it? No. This branch cannot teach us anything and cannot catch bad input: Python still treats strings, numbers, None, and other values as truthy or falsy, so one of the three earlier conditions will catch them.

The better defensive habit: validate incoming data before the decision tree when its type or meaning matters. Add an else only when the program has a meaningful action for everything not already matched. Keep that scope in mind as you write compound conditions — not is a precise tool that reaches exactly one step to its right. Brackets: Bundling A Question Before You Join It To Another

You have just seen and and not working together in one condition. Gate 2 is about to go one step further — mixing and and or in the same line. Before that happens, there is one small tool worth picking up, because without it that kind of line becomes very hard to read: brackets.

Think about a thermostat. A heating system might be told: switch on if the room is cold, and either it is daytime or someone is home. Daytime and someone-home are not two separate requirements sitting side by side with cold — they are one bundled question: "is there a reason to bother heating the place right now?" Either daytime or occupied is enough to answer that bundle yes. Cold is a completely separate requirement that has nothing to do with that bundle.

Without brackets, that line becomes genuinely hard to read — your eye loses track of which parts belong together. Brackets solve that by drawing a box around the bundle. Python answers what is inside the box first, gets one yes or no out of it, and only then joins that answer to whatever is outside the box:

is_cold = True is_daytime = False someone_home = True if is_cold and (is_daytime or someone_home): print("Heating on")

Read it as: is it cold? AND — answering the bundle first — is it daytime or is someone home? The bracket resolves the bundle into one yes/no before the and even gets asked. In this case: cold is True, daytime is False but someone is home so the bundle is True, and True and True fires the gate. The heating comes on.

That is the whole job of brackets here: bundle a smaller question into one unit before it gets joined to anything else. You reach for them the moment a line mixes and and or together — draw a box around the or part, and the line reads cleanly.

This is the same principle as distance_mi <= 6 back in the travel gates — you only ever ask the upper boundary, because the earlier branches have already handled everything below it.

The Fallback: Just One Of Them

If none of the three gates above fired, only one possibility can remain: exactly one person is on the porch. No condition is needed — the else catches whatever the sandwich has not already handled.

else: print("Only one of them is here")

Nothing left to check. The sandwich is closed.

One Thing Before We Begin: The Sandwich Has Two Levels

Every exercise so far has asked one question per gate — one if line, one thing to check, one answer. Bob's Travel App is different. Each distance chapter in the sandwich has its own smaller question living inside it, and that smaller question has its own if/else block nested underneath the chapter line.

Here is the full outer sandwich — all four gates, no inner questions yet:

if not distance_mi: # Gate 1: no usable distance — stop here print(False) elif distance_mi <= 1: # Gate 2: short trip — open short-trip chapter print("short trip chapter") elif distance_mi <= 6: # Gate 3: medium trip — open medium-trip chapter print("medium trip chapter") else: # Gate 4: long trip — open long-trip chapter print("long trip chapter")

This is the outer sandwich. It only does one job: choose which chapter to open. It does not yet ask whether Bob can actually travel. That is the inner question — and it lives inside each chapter, indented underneath the chapter selector line. Your job is to fill in each chapter's inner question. Here is the structure to work from:

if not distance_mi: print(False) elif distance_mi <= 1: # Gate 2 inner question: what single condition decides if Bob can walk? # If the condition is met: print True # If not: print False elif distance_mi <= 6: # Gate 3 inner question: what two conditions must both be true for Bob to take the bike? # If both are met: print True # If not: print False else: # Gate 4 inner question: what single condition (with two possible routes) lets Bob travel? # If the condition is met: print True # If not: print False

The outer elif line selects the chapter. The inner question decides the answer. Keep them on separate lines — one indented inside the other. That separation is the whole structure of the app.

The Actual App Rules

Bob's Travel App asks us to decide whether commuting is possible. The rules map directly onto the four gates — and since you already know what a comment spine looks like, here they are written as one:

Distance is the organiser. The main structure is not rain, bike, car, or ride-share — those are the inner questions that live inside each chapter. The main structure is distance, distance, distance, distance. Distance chooses the chapter. The chapter tells us which smaller question to ask next.

These comments are the compartments. They stop the whole app from becoming one tangled thought. Each one says: this is the question this part of the code is responsible for.

Why The Distance Gates Go In This Order

The gates go from smallest to largest: no usable distance, 1 mile or less, up to 6 miles, greater than 6 miles. That order matters because Python reads an if / elif / elif / else sandwich from top to bottom — once one branch matches, it runs and the rest are skipped.

The key one is Gate 3. The rule says "greater than 1 mile and up to 6 miles" — but the code only asks elif distance_mi <= 6. It does not need to ask "greater than 1" because Gate 2 already caught everything up to and including 1. By the time Python reaches Gate 3, it is already in a world where the distance is more than 1. The sandwich has done the lower-boundary work — same principle as every earlier elif you have written. You only ever ask the upper boundary.

A question always comes before a variable. We did not start writing distance_mi until we already knew, in plain English, what we were trying to find out. Code without a question behind it is just syntax looking for a job.

Positive interrogation is not a quirk to memorise, it is the actual shape of every Python check. There is no built-in word for "no" — there is only "yes," aimed at whatever case you care about. if not is not a special trick. It is the same turnstile, asked about absence instead of presence.

A gate asks one question and gets out of the way. String enough gates together, in the right order, and a problem that looked tangled becomes a sequence of single, simple decisions. Get the order wrong — ask a detail before the thing that makes the detail matter — and the whole structure either breaks or quietly lies to you, the way the vending machine did when it took the coin and kept the chocolate.

The order itself carries meaning. By the time code reaches an elif, every earlier gate has already failed, and that failure narrows what the elif actually means without a single word of new code. distance_mi <= 6 only means "between 1 and 6" because distance_mi <= 1 already came and went. Read gates in isolation and you miss this. Read them in sequence and the sandwich does half the thinking for you.

and and or are not interchangeable tools for joining ideas, they answer two different shapes of real-world question. and is the checklist: every part has to hold, or the whole thing fails, the way a car will not start on a seatbelt alone. or is the tripwire: one trigger is enough, the way a smoke alarm does not wait for permission from a second sensor. Knowing which shape you are looking at, before you write the line, is the actual skill — the keyword just records the decision you already made in your head.

Round brackets—called parentheses—bundle a question before it gets handed to another question. When and and or share a line, parentheses make the intended grouping visible instead of asking the reader to remember Python's operator precedence.
